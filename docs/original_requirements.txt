1. Executive Summary
Vantara Retail Solutions operates an omnichannel retail and e-commerce business serving both direct consumers and small business resellers. Over the past three years, the volume of transactional data captured through the order management system, loyalty program, and web analytics stack has grown substantially, but this data is not yet being used to drive proactive, customer-level decisions.
This document specifies the requirements for a Customer Behavior Prediction Platform — an internal analytics system that ingests historical transaction data and produces churn risk scores, customer lifetime value estimates, next-purchase probability, product recommendations, and customer segments. The platform will be delivered as a working prototype with a REST API, an interactive dashboard, and full documentation, built end-to-end by a single data scientist over a four-week sprint.
The platform is intended to serve as both a functioning internal analytics tool for the Marketing and Retention teams and a reference implementation demonstrating the organization's applied machine learning capability. It is designed to be handed over to a new team member or a rotational trainee as a self-contained, well-specified assignment.
2. Business Problem & Motivation
2.1 Problem Statement
The Retention and Marketing teams currently rely on manual, rule-based segmentation (for example, “customers who haven’t ordered in 90 days”) to decide who receives discount campaigns, re-engagement emails, or loyalty offers. This approach is reactive, treats all customers within a segment identically, and does not account for the underlying purchase patterns that predict whether a customer is actually at risk of churning or likely to respond to an offer.
Without a predictive system, the business faces three recurring issues:
Marketing spend is distributed evenly across at-risk customers instead of being prioritized by predicted value and predicted churn probability, reducing campaign ROI.
High-value customers who show early warning signs of disengagement are identified too late, typically after they have already stopped purchasing for several months.
Product recommendations shown on the website and in email campaigns are based on simple “best-seller” logic rather than individual purchase history, resulting in low click-through and conversion rates.
2.2 Motivation
Retail and e-commerce margins are increasingly sensitive to customer acquisition cost. Retaining an existing customer is significantly cheaper than acquiring a new one, which makes early, accurate churn prediction and lifetime value estimation directly tied to profitability. A single, well-engineered predictive platform can replace several disconnected manual reports currently produced by the analytics team on an ad-hoc basis.
2.3 Real-World Applications
Prioritized retention campaigns targeted at high-value, high-churn-risk customers.
Dynamic discount allocation based on predicted discount sensitivity rather than blanket promotions.
Inventory and demand planning informed by predicted purchase timing and category demand.
Personalized product recommendations embedded in the marketing email pipeline.
Executive reporting on cohort-level revenue forecasts and customer health.

4. Scope of Work
4.1 In Scope
End-to-end pipeline from raw transaction-level CSV data to customer-level feature table.
Classical machine learning models: Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and one distance-based model (KNN or SVM) for churn classification.
One deep learning classifier (feed-forward ANN) trained on the same feature set for comparison against classical models.
One sequence model (LSTM) trained on time-ordered purchase sequences to model purchase timing/recency behavior.
One autoencoder for unsupervised anomaly detection on spending patterns.
Customer segmentation using K-Means and one additional algorithm (DBSCAN or Gaussian Mixture Models).
SHAP-based explainability for the best-performing churn model, plus at least one LIME example for a single customer.
A FastAPI service exposing prediction endpoints and a Streamlit dashboard consuming that service.
Containerization with Docker and a documented local deployment procedure.
4.2 Out of Scope (Explicitly Deferred)
Transformer-based sequence models — documented as a future enhancement rather than a Week 1–4 deliverable, given the timeline.
Real-time streaming ingestion (Kafka/event pipelines) — the platform will operate on scheduled batch scoring for this phase.
Multi-tenant authentication, role-based access control, and production-grade CI/CD — noted as future hardening work.
A/B testing framework for measuring the actual business impact of the model's recommendations in live campaigns.
Scoping these items out is a deliberate decision to keep the project achievable by one engineer in four weeks while still covering the full ML/DL lifecycle at a meaningful depth rather than superficially touching every possible technique.

5.2 Supplementary Dataset (Optional Enrichment)
For richer demographic and behavioral features, the Customer Personality Analysis dataset (Kaggle) can optionally be used as a secondary, independently-trained module to demonstrate handling of a dataset that already includes engineered demographic fields (income, family composition, campaign response history). This is treated as a stretch goal, not a core dependency, since it does not share customer keys with the transactional dataset and must be modeled separately.
5.3 Known Data Quality Issues
A meaningful share of invoice line items have a missing Customer ID and must be excluded from customer-level modeling (they remain usable for product-level analysis only).
Negative Quantity values represent returns/cancellations and must be handled explicitly rather than dropped, since return behavior is itself a predictive signal.
A small number of unit prices are zero or negative, corresponding to adjustments and manual corrections, and require a documented cleaning rule.
StockCode contains some non-product administrative codes (postage, bank charges, manual entries) that must be filtered out of product affinity features.
InvoiceDate spans a leap year and multiple holiday seasons, which must be accounted for when engineering seasonal features.
5.4 Why This Dataset Is Appropriate
The dataset is real, publicly documented, large enough to support customer-level aggregation with a statistically meaningful number of repeat customers, and messy in realistic ways (missing IDs, returns, inconsistent descriptions). This makes it suitable for demonstrating genuine data cleaning and feature engineering skill, rather than working with a dataset that is already analysis-ready.

6. Functional Requirements – Data Pipeline
6.1 Data Collection
The pipeline must load both sheets of the source workbook, standardize column names and types, and combine them into a single chronologically ordered transaction table before any cleaning logic is applied.
6.2 Data Cleaning
Remove or explicitly flag rows with missing Customer ID depending on downstream use (product-level vs customer-level tables).
Deduplicate exact duplicate invoice lines while preserving legitimate repeated purchases of the same SKU in the same order.
Detect outliers in Quantity and Price using IQR-based bounds and domain rules (e.g., bulk wholesale orders), and separate true data-entry errors from legitimate bulk purchases.
Standardize inconsistent product descriptions (case, whitespace, known typos) using a lookup table keyed on StockCode.
Implement automated data validation checks (schema checks, null-rate thresholds, date-range checks) that run before the feature engineering stage and fail loudly if violated.
6.3 Data Wrangling
All wrangling must be implemented in Pandas and NumPy as versioned, unit-testable functions rather than inline notebook cells, so the same logic can run in both the training pipeline and the live scoring API.
Categorical encoding: one-hot encoding for low-cardinality fields (Country), target/frequency encoding for high-cardinality fields (StockCode-derived features).
Numerical scaling: StandardScaler for models sensitive to feature scale (Logistic Regression, KNN, SVM, neural networks); tree-based models trained on unscaled features.
Persist the fitted encoders and scalers as artifacts so the exact same transformation is applied at inference time.
6.4 Exploratory Data Analysis
Univariate and bivariate statistical summaries of spend, frequency, and recency distributions.
Correlation analysis between engineered features and the churn/CLV targets, with multicollinearity checked via VIF before finalizing the feature set.
Country-level and seasonal purchase distribution analysis.
A documented EDA notebook that ends with a written list of hypotheses the modeling stage will test.
7. Functional Requirements – Feature Engineering
The following customer-level features must be computed and documented with a one-line justification for why each feature contributes predictive signal.
Feature
Why It Matters
Recency (days since last purchase)
Directly precedes churn; the single strongest univariate churn predictor in retail data.
Frequency (number of distinct orders)
Distinguishes habitual buyers from one-time purchasers.
Monetary value (total and average spend)
Core input for both churn risk weighting and CLV.
Customer Lifetime Value (historical + predicted)
Lets the business prioritize retention spend by expected return, not just churn risk.
Average basket size
Signals shopping behavior type (top-up vs. bulk shopper).
Purchase frequency trend (slope over time)
Captures acceleration or deceleration in engagement that a static average would hide.
Time between consecutive purchases (variance)
High variance indicates irregular, harder-to-predict customers.
Seasonal purchase concentration
Distinguishes gift-driven or seasonal shoppers from year-round customers.
Product category affinity vector
Feeds the recommendation logic and improves next-category prediction.
Return rate
A known leading indicator of dissatisfaction and future churn.
Discount/markdown sensitivity
Identifies which customers should receive promotional offers versus full-price marketing.
Engagement score (composite of recency, frequency, monetary)
A single interpretable score for dashboard consumption by non-technical stakeholders.
All engineered features must be computed with a point-in-time cutoff discipline: any feature used to predict an event must only use data available strictly before the prediction date, to avoid target leakage. This is a common and serious error in churn modeling and must be explicitly tested for in the pipeline's unit tests.

8.2 Deep Learning Models
Artificial Neural Network (ANN): a feed-forward network on the same engineered feature set as the classical models, used as a direct accuracy/AUC comparison point. Must include batch normalization, dropout, and early stopping on validation loss.
LSTM: trained on time-ordered sequences of each customer's purchase events (amount, gap-in-days, category) to model sequential purchase behavior and improve next-purchase-timing prediction beyond what static features capture.
Autoencoder: trained on scaled spending-pattern features in an unsupervised manner; reconstruction error above a validated threshold flags anomalous accounts for manual review (potential fraud, data entry errors, or extreme high-value outliers).
A Transformer-based sequence model is documented in Section 4.2 as an explicit future enhancement rather than a required deliverable, since a well-tuned Transformer typically needs a larger sequence dataset and more tuning time than a four-week solo sprint allows.
8.3 Model Selection Discipline
All models must be trained with a fixed random seed and a stratified train/validation/test split (recommended 70/15/15) to keep comparisons fair.
Hyperparameter search must use cross-validation on the training set only; the test set is touched exactly once, at the end, for final reporting.
Every model run must be logged (parameters, metrics, training time) so the final comparison table in Section 11 can be regenerated from logs rather than re-typed by hand.
9. Functional Requirements – Customer Segmentation
K-Means: primary segmentation method; determine k using the elbow method and silhouette score, not an arbitrary fixed number.
DBSCAN or Gaussian Mixture Models (one required): used specifically to test whether K-Means' spherical-cluster assumption is hiding irregularly shaped or overlapping segments.
Hierarchical clustering may be used as an exploratory dendrogram-based sanity check on a sampled subset of customers, given its poor scalability to the full customer base.
Each resulting segment must be given a business-readable profile (e.g., “high-frequency low-value,” “lapsed high-value,” “seasonal gift shopper”) with summary statistics, not just a cluster number, since the Marketing team is the end consumer of this output.
10. Functional Requirements – Explainability (XAI)
SHAP: global feature importance summary plot for the production churn model, plus SHAP force plots for at least three representative individual customers (one low-risk, one high-risk, one borderline).
LIME: at least one worked example showing a local explanation for a single prediction, compared against the SHAP explanation for the same customer to illustrate agreement or divergence.
Partial Dependence Plots for the two or three most influential features (typically recency and frequency) to show the direction and shape of their effect on predicted churn probability.
A plain-language “why this customer is flagged” explanation template that translates SHAP values into a sentence a marketing analyst can read without any ML background.

14. System Architecture
At a high level, the system is organized into four layers: a data layer (raw files and PostgreSQL), a processing layer (the Pandas/NumPy pipeline and feature store), a modeling layer (trained model artifacts for classical ML, deep learning, and clustering), and a serving layer (the FastAPI service and the Streamlit dashboard that consumes it). Batch scoring jobs write predictions back into PostgreSQL, which both the API and dashboard read from, so the dashboard never needs to call the model directly.
Deliverable: a one-page architecture diagram (data flow from raw CSV to dashboard) and a simple ER diagram for the PostgreSQL schema (customers, transactions, predictions, segments tables) must be produced during Week 4 documentation and included in the final submission as image files in the docs/ directory.

16. Mathematical Foundations Reference
The final report must include a short appendix (2–4 pages) grounding the implementation in the underlying mathematics. This is not a separate research task — it should be written as the models are built, referencing the actual equations used in the code.
16.1 Statistics & Probability
RFM and engagement scoring rely on descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, percentile ranks) and on conditional probability when estimating next-purchase probability, P(purchase | recency, frequency, monetary), which the classification models approximate directly through their predicted probability output.
16.2 Linear Algebra & PCA
Feature matrices are represented as X ∈ ℝ^(n×d). Principal Component Analysis, used optionally for visualization of customer segments in two dimensions, finds the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix Σ = (1/n) XᵀX that capture the directions of maximum variance.
16.3 Logistic Regression & Cross-Entropy
The logistic regression baseline models P(y=1|x) = 1 / (1 + e^−(wᵀx+b)), trained by minimizing the binary cross-entropy loss L = −(1/n) Σ [yᵢ log(ŷᵢ) + (1−yᵢ) log(1−ŷᵢ)].
16.4 Gradient Descent & Backpropagation
Neural network weights are updated iteratively as w ← w − η ∇L(w), where η is the learning rate. Backpropagation computes ∇L(w) via the chain rule through each layer of the ANN and LSTM, and the report must show the loss curve (training vs. validation) used to confirm convergence and detect overfitting.
16.5 Regularization
L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) penalty terms are added to the logistic regression and neural network loss functions to discourage overfitting; dropout in the ANN and LSTM serves an analogous regularizing role by randomly deactivating units during training.
16.6 Evaluation Metric Definitions
Precision = TP/(TP+FP), Recall = TP/(TP+FN), F1 = 2·(Precision·Recall)/(Precision+Recall), and ROC-AUC summarizes the trade-off between true positive rate and false positive rate across all classification thresholds rather than a single fixed cutoff.

19. Risks & Mitigations
Risk
Mitigation
Only ~5,900 unique customers limits deep learning model performance
Use the ANN and LSTM primarily as comparative/learning exercises; rely on gradient-boosted trees as the production recommendation, and state this trade-off explicitly in the report.
Class imbalance in churn labels
Use class-weighting or SMOTE on the training set only (never on validation/test), and report recall/F1/AUC rather than accuracy.
Target leakage in engineered features
Enforce point-in-time cutoff discipline (Section 7) and add explicit leakage unit tests before model training begins.
Four-week timeline pressure on deep learning + deployment in the same phase
Week 3 and Week 4 scopes are intentionally fixed in Section 17; if behind schedule, drop the Transformer stretch goal and CatBoost comparison first — they are explicitly non-mandatory.
Single point of failure (one engineer, no code review)
Daily checkpoint deliverables (Section 17) allow a manager or mentor to review progress incrementally rather than only at final handover.
